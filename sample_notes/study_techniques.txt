Study Techniques — Notes

Active Recall
Active recall means testing yourself on material instead of just re-reading it.
Close the book and try to write down everything you remember about a topic,
then check what you missed. This is far more effective than highlighting or
passively rereading notes, because retrieving information strengthens memory
more than simply recognizing it.

Spaced Repetition
Spaced repetition schedules review sessions at increasing intervals — for
example, review a topic after one day, then three days, then a week, then two
weeks. Each successful recall pushes the next review further out. This fights
the natural forgetting curve, where memory of new material decays fastest in
the first 24 hours after learning it.

The Feynman Technique
To check whether you truly understand something, try explaining it in plain
language as if teaching it to a twelve-year-old. Write the explanation down,
and any time you get stuck or fall back on jargon, that's exactly the gap in
your understanding you need to go back and fix.

Interleaving
Instead of practicing one type of problem repeatedly in a single block (e.g.
all addition problems, then all subtraction problems), interleaving mixes
different types of problems together in one study session. It feels harder
in the moment and slows down initial practice, but produces much better
long-term retention and the ability to tell problem types apart.

Pomodoro Technique
Work in focused 25-minute blocks followed by a 5-minute break. After four
blocks, take a longer 15-30 minute break. The short, bounded intervals make
starting a task feel less daunting and help maintain concentration by
treating breaks as a built-in reward rather than a distraction.
